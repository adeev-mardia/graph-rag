The Space Race between the Soviet Union and the United States unfolded against the backdrop of the broader Cold War, a geopolitical rivalry that shaped nearly every major decision in both countries' space programs. President Dwight Eisenhower initially viewed spaceflight mainly through a military lens, but the launch of Sputnik 1 forced him to create NASA as a civilian agency to compete openly with the Soviet Union. President John F. Kennedy later used the Apollo program as a demonstration of American technological superiority over the Soviet Union, a message aimed as much at the Third World as at the Soviet leadership itself. Sergei Korolev's identity was kept a state secret by the Soviet government throughout his life, and he was only publicly identified as the chief architect of the Soviet space program after his death in 1966. The Cold War rivalry that drove the Apollo program and the Soviet Vostok and Soyuz programs gradually gave way to cooperation, culminating in the Apollo-Soyuz Test Project of 1975, in which an American Apollo spacecraft docked with a Soviet Soyuz spacecraft in orbit. That cooperative spirit eventually led to the joint construction of the International Space Station between NASA and Roscosmos.
